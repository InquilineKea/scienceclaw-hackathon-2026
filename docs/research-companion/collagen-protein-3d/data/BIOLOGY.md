# What collagen–protein connections mean in this study

Collagen–decorin and collagen–fibronectin interactions are meaningful targets, but their mechanical roles cannot be reduced to adding interchangeable permanent springs. Binding, covalent linking, and changes during matrix assembly need different experiments and different model controls.

| Mechanism | Evidence and interpretation | Representation here | Remaining gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decorin binding to collagen | Mutagenesis and oligomerization experiments support collagen binding through decorin’s concave face and binding by the monomer. This supports specific recognition rather than an arbitrary generic crosslink. | Designated noncovalent contacts with a finite energy well; a two-site bridge is a deliberately simplified mechanical proxy. | The model has no decorin sequence, core-protein surface, glycosaminoglycan chain, measured binding-site density, or dissociation kinetics. Two arms do not establish native molecular valency. |
| Decorin changing collagen assembly | In reconstituted type-I collagen gels, decorin present during fibrillogenesis reduced aggregation and increased modulus; adding decorin afterward had no stiffening effect. This argues for testing assembly history separately from direct load transfer. | Organization endpoints vary independently of contact laws, and every organization has an adapter-free control. | Prescribed point clustering and connection alignment do not simulate fibrillogenesis or prove the direction of a biological decorin effect. |
| Fibronectin binding to collagen | A structural study identified collagen-I peptide binding to fibronectin gelatin-binding-domain fragments. Its peptide conformation also cautions against assuming all collagen surfaces are equivalently accessible. | Finite-depth contacts on fixed sites in a multivalent adapter assembly. | A four-arm adapter represents a small assembly, not the stoichiometry of one fibronectin molecule. Native accessibility, unfolding, and specificity are absent. |
| Fibronectin supporting matrix assembly | In fibronectin-null cell experiments, fibronectin polymerization supported collagen-I deposition and retention in fibrillar matrix. | An organization intervention is replayed with and without protein adapters. | No cells, integrins, secretion, traction, turnover, or fibronectin polymerization kinetics are simulated. |
| Covalent collagen–fibronectin links | Factor XIIIa was shown to crosslink fibronectin to collagen I/III in vitro under specified assay conditions. This is evidence for a real possible heterotypic covalent connection. | A breakable locked contact is compared with binding on the exact same sites, matching initial stiffness and per-arm energy budget. | The harmonic law is not a parameterized Factor-XIIIa reaction or chemical bond. It does not establish in-vivo abundance. Locked decorin is only a counterfactual control. |

The strongest experimental follow-up would cross protein identity and assembly timing: collagen alone; protein present during assembly; the same protein added after assembly; and a covalent-link condition with an appropriate enzyme-negative control. Measure architecture alongside mechanics: orientation, fibril diameter and bundling, pore distribution, and connectivity. Match collagen concentration, quantify actual protein occupancy, and distinguish reversible association from covalent incorporation with an appropriate biochemical assay. That would allow the computational organization and contact controls to be tied to measured inputs.

The present GCP study is a mechanism sensitivity calculation in reduced units. It can show that an assumed architecture or interaction law changes a model response and whether that effect persists across graph seeds and two volumes. It cannot determine which protein makes real tissue stronger, estimate physiological bond energies, or reproduce an assembly pathway.
